Which Pages Should an Editor Refresh First? A Learned Ranking vs a Hand-Written Rule on Held-Out Search Data

Author: Quang Pham (ML Intern, FlyRank) Date: November 2026 Track: Lane 2 — Refresh / Opportunity Scoring Data: FlyRank ML Internship starter slice — 30,000 pages, 32 clients

Abstract

FlyRank publishes and monitors content across many client websites, where pages that once ranked can quietly lose search visibility while editors can review only a few dozen a week, so the useful question is which pages to look at first: can a model trained on observable search and engagement signals rank pages that are losing impressions above a transparent hand-written rule, on clients it has never seen? We used the FlyRank ML Internship starter slice (30,000 anonymized pages from 32 clients, trailing 90-day Google Search Console and GA4 metrics) and labelled a page as declining when its last-30-day impressions fell more than 20% versus the previous 30 days. A gradient-boosted classifier was compared with our Week 4 striking-distance rule on identical client-grouped test sets, repeated over 20 different client splits so that no single lucky split carries the result. Across those splits the model's top-10 queue averaged 89% declining pages (range 80–100%) against 40% for the rule, and its top-50 averaged 81% against 47%, beating the rule on every split. Part of that gain comes from 90-day features that overlap the label window: using only data from before it, the model still leads (top-10 72%, top-50 68%, ROC-AUC 0.63), but by less. We turn the scores into a ranked, reason-coded review queue for human editors: a decision-support tool for where to look first, not evidence that a refresh will cause recovery.

1. Introduction & Problem Statement

Picture a content team with 20,000 published pages and time to review 20 of them this week. Each slot spent on a page that is fine is a slot not spent on one that is slipping. The decision this work supports is narrow and concrete: which pages go into this week's review queue, and in what order.

The FlyRank case

FlyRank researches, writes and publishes content directly into client websites, then watches Google Search Console and GA4 data to keep that content performing. Across many clients and thousands of pages, the recurring problem is decay: a page ranks, then rankings slip and clicks fall, and it is easy to notice too late.

Today, pages are flagged for attention with hand-written rules: a health score, quick-win tags, needs-attention flags. These are if-this-then-that rules with hand-picked thresholds. They are transparent and they run in production, but rules get harder to tune as signals multiply and interact. This paper asks whether a trained model can order the review queue better than a rule of that kind.

One boundary matters. FlyRank's production flags are deliberately not in the internship data, so this paper does not compare against them. The baseline is our own Week 4 rule, built in the same style from the same observable signals. The results show what a model adds over a rule of that kind. They are not a verdict on FlyRank's product.

Why a rule is not enough

Our Week 4 baseline encoded a common SEO intuition: review pages in "striking distance" (average position 4–20) that are consistently visible, ordered by impressions. The ordering is the weak point. It pushes the largest pages to the top, and large, steady pages are often not the ones losing ground. On the held-out clients used in Week 5, the rule's top 10 contained only 2 declining pages, fewer than picking at random would give.

Research question. On clients the model has never seen, does a model trained on observable 90-day search and engagement signals rank declining pages above the Week 4 rule, and does that hold across many different client splits rather than just one?

2. Data

Which data, stated plainly

  • Used for every model and number here: the anonymized starter slice of the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, content_refresh_anonymized.csv. It is one table with 30,000 rows × 44 columns, one row per content item, from 32 pseudonymized clients. It combines Google Search Console visibility with GA4 engagement.
  • Not used for modeling: the ~79M-row warehouse release (flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703). It was only queried for the Week 3 data contract. Re-earning these results on it is future work.

Date windows and label

  • Features are aggregates over a trailing 90-day window ending at export time. The starter slice does not publish calendar dates.
  • The label compares impressions in the last 30 days with the 30 days before that. A page is declining (trend_direction = 'down') when the change is below −20%.
  • Base rate: 54.2% of all pages (16,262 of 30,000) are declining. On individual 7-client test sets it ranges from 25% to 61%, which is why every result below is shown next to its own base rate.

What we excluded, and why

  • trend_direction, trend_pct: they define the label.
  • All *_last_30d and *_prev_30d columns: the label is computed from them.
  • client_id, content_id: pseudonyms used only to group the train/test split, never as features.
  • provider_used, model_used: tooling metadata, not search evidence.

The slice contains no client names, domains, URLs, page titles or search queries, and this page reports only aggregates.

3. Methodology

Features and assumption

We used 24 features: 18 numeric (log traffic, CTR, active days, average position, keyword volume, CPC and competition, content age, days since update, word count, engagement and scroll rates, AI-referral share, plus has_ flags for missing values) and 6 categorical buckets (content type, intent, competition level, freshness, position tier and word-count tier). The working assumption is that a page's 90-day profile carries information about whether its last 30 days look worse than the 30 before. The windows overlap in time, so this tests association within one snapshot, not a forecast of a later period.

Baseline: the Week 4 rule, unchanged

score = [4 ≤ position ≤ 20] × [active days ≥ 45] × [impressions ≥ 300] × impressions_90d

Pages that pass all three gates are ordered by impressions, and every other page scores 0.

Model

HistGradientBoostingClassifier (max depth 5, min leaf 25, balanced class weights) after scaling numeric features and one-hot encoding categories. Logistic regression is reported alongside as a simpler reference.

Validation design

  1. Client-grouped holdout. Twenty percent of clients are held out (GroupShuffleSplit on client_id), so every test page comes from a client the model never saw. This mirrors onboarding a new client.
  2. Repeated over 20 client splits. A test set holds only 7 clients, and the model family was chosen in Week 5 on one such split. Repeating the full comparison over 20 different client splits shows the spread and reduces dependence on that one split.
  3. Same test pages for every method. The model and the rule are always scored on identical pages, next to that split's base rate.

Leakage checks

  • A code-level guard fails the run if any label source, 30-day window component or ID appears in the feature list.
  • Positive control (Week 6): injecting the label arithmetic on purpose pushes AUC to ≈1.000, so the harness can detect leakage. The clean pipeline scores far below that.
  • Random-row vs client-grouped comparison (Section 4.3) measures how much seeing the same client in training inflates scores.
  • Overlapping windows (found in the ML-05 leakage check). The 90-day features include the last 30 days that define the label. None of them is the label, but they partly describe the same days. So every result is also reported for a strict model. It sees only traffic from days 61–90 (90-day total minus the last and previous 30 days, entirely before the label window) plus static page and keyword fields (Section 4.4).

4.3 Random row splits overstate performance

In Week 6 we reported a 0.163 AUC gap (0.783 random vs 0.620 grouped). That figure paired one random split with the hard seed-42 client split. Averaged over many splits, the inflation is about 0.075 AUC. That is still large enough that a random split would mislead anyone onboarding a new client.

4.4 Part of the gain comes from overlapping windows

A page's 90-day traffic includes the same last 30 days that decide its label. To measure how much that helps, the same model was retrained on strict features: traffic from days 61–90 only, plus static page and keyword fields, all known before the label window starts.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Starter slice, not the warehouse. All results come from 30,000 pages and 32 clients. They have not been reproduced on the ~79M-row warehouse release.
  • One snapshot, overlapping windows, not a forecast. Features and label come from the same export, and the 90-day features overlap the label window. With strictly earlier features, top-10 precision drops from 0.89 to 0.72 (Section 4.4). The model ranks pages that look like recent decliners and has not been tested on a later, separate time period.
  • Decision support, not causal lift. A high score means "this page resembles pages that lost impressions", not "refreshing it will bring traffic back". No intervention was tested.
  • Few clients per test. Each test set holds 7 clients, and a top 10 is 10 pages. That is why we report means and ranges over 20 splits.
  • Model chosen with a test split in view. Gradient boosting was picked in Week 5 on the seed-42 split. Repeating over 20 splits reduces this optimism but does not remove it. A clean answer needs clients that were never looked at.
  • Moderate overall ranking. A mean ROC-AUC of 0.70 means the model is much stronger at the very top of the queue than across the whole catalog. Use it to order a short review list, not to label every page.
  • Noisy label. Small pages cross ±20% through ordinary fluctuation, so a "declining" label is not always a real problem.
  • Catalog-wide scores are partly in-sample. The Week 7 queue scores all 30,000 pages, including pages from the clients the model was trained on. Only the held-out results above count as evaluation. The action mix in Figure 3 is descriptive.
  • Reason codes are descriptive, not explanatory. In the top-20 review, correct and incorrect picks carried the same codes. They tell an editor what to look at, not why the model flagged the page.
  • No inside knowledge of Google. These are observed associations in one portfolio's GSC and GA4 data. They say nothing about how Google ranks pages.

6. Ranked Recommendations & Action Playbook

From probability to a review queue

Sorting by decline probability alone surfaces tiny pages with little traffic at stake. The Week 7 playbook blends three signals into a 0–100 priority. The weights are a judgment call, not fitted:

priority = 100 × [ 0.60 × P(decline) + 0.25 × ln(1+impressions) / max ln(1+impressions) + 0.15 × position leverage ]

Position leverage is 1.00 for positions 4–10, 0.90 for 10–20, 0.60 for 1–3, 0.40 for 20–50, 0.20 beyond 50, and 0.15 for pages with no position data. The blend costs some precision. On the seed-42 held-out clients, the queue's top 50 was 66% declining, against 80% for the raw model and 42% for the rule. In exchange, it surfaces pages with more traffic at stake.

A by-hand look at the top 20

Metrics can hide what kind of pages a queue puts first, so the top 20 of the queue on the held-out clients were reviewed one by one, using coarse buckets only. 16 of 20 were really declining, against a base rate of 0.51. The 4 misses were stable or growing pages, not borderline noise. Three things stood out:

  • One client took 16 of the 20 slots, and only 2 of the 7 held-out clients appeared at all. A single queue across clients would hand one team almost all the work.
  • The top is one-note. 19 of 20 pages got the same action (snippet/CTR refresh) and hits and misses carried the same reason codes. The codes describe the page, but they do not explain why the model rates it risky.
  • Rank 1 was a miss, a stable page with 100k+ impressions lifted by the traffic weight in the priority formula. That is the volume trap again, in a smaller dose.

What to do first

  1. Start each week from the top of the queue, not from the biggest pages. Across 20 client splits, the model's top 10 held 72–89% declining pages, depending on how strictly features are cut off before the label window. The impressions-ordered rule held about 40%.
  2. Try the cheap fix first on striking-distance pages. Pages at positions 4–20 with CTR below 0.35% get a title and snippet rewrite before anything heavier.
  3. Leave strong top-10 pages alone unless they show their own warning signs.
  4. Measure every refresh against similar pages that were not refreshed, over 30–60 days. That is the only way to turn this ranking into evidence of impact.
  5. Build the queue per client (or per editor team), so one client's many similar pages do not crowd out everyone else.

What this means for FlyRank's refresh workflow

  1. Use the model to order the review queue, not to replace review. Editors still decide. The model decides what they look at first, and every entry shows its reason codes.
  2. Run it next to the existing flags before trusting it. Score the same pages with both, then compare how many of each top 20 turn out to be declining over the following weeks. That is the comparison this paper could not make.
  3. Re-earn the result forward in time. The warehouse release has daily data. Training on earlier months and testing on later ones, on unseen clients, would turn this from "looks like a decliner" into a real forecast.
  4. Close the loop. Log which flagged pages were refreshed and how they moved over the next 30–60 days compared with similar pages that were not. Over time that measures the impact of refreshing, which this paper cannot claim.
